\vspace{0.5cm}

\noindent
\textbf{Abstract}

\vspace{0.2cm}

\begin{justify}

Gamification is increasingly used in education to improve student engagement, motivation, participation, satisfaction, and learning outcomes. This review integrates recent peer-reviewed research published between 2022 and 2025, including theoretical work on Self-Determination Theory, a meta-analysis of collaborative learning, design-based research on fantasy-enhanced gamification, case studies in higher education, and empirical studies involving artificial intelligence, web development, vocational learning software, and graduate e-learning. Across the reviewed literature, gamification is generally associated with positive educational outcomes, but the effects are not uniform. Some studies report clear improvements in academic performance, engagement, or satisfaction, whereas others show that performance may improve without statistically significant gains in engagement or motivation. The evidence therefore suggests that the effectiveness of gamification depends on design quality, learner characteristics, educational context, implementation duration, and the extent to which game elements are aligned with learning objectives. This review concludes that gamification can enhance education and learning when it is implemented as a purposeful pedagogical strategy rather than as a simple reward system, while also identifying the need for longitudinal, comparative, and more context-sensitive research.

\vspace{0.3cm}

\textbf{Keywords:} Gamification; Education; Learning; Student Engagement; Motivation; Academic Performance

\end{justify}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Background}

\textbf{Gamification} refers to the application of game elements and game-design principles in non-game contexts. In education, commonly used elements include points, badges, leaderboards, levels, challenges, rewards, narratives, progress tracking, and immediate feedback. Recent research shows that these elements are being integrated into a wide range of learning environments, including higher education, collaborative learning, vocational education, web development courses, e-learning, and AI-supported learning systems \parencite{gao2024,slamet2025,dahri2025aigamification,ojonuba2025webgamification,wang2024gamification,sevillacampoverde2025elearning}.

A major theoretical explanation for the effectiveness of gamification is Self-Determination Theory (SDT). Gao argues that gamification should not be understood simply as adding rewards; instead, its motivational effect depends on how learners interpret game elements and whether the design supports autonomy, competence, and relatedness \parencite{gao2024}. This theoretical perspective is consistent with empirical studies that use clear goals, feedback, challenges, progression, collaboration, and personalized learning to improve the learning experience \parencite{ojonuba2025webgamification,sevillacampoverde2025elearning,bai2022fantasy}.

Research also demonstrates that gamification can be implemented in different ways. Slamet and Meng synthesized 35 studies of collaborative learning and found substantial positive effects on both cognitive and attitudinal outcomes \parencite{slamet2025}. Alonso-S\'anchez et al. examined 354 higher-education students and found that students generally perceived gamification as beneficial for learning, academic results, participation, and motivation \parencite{alonsosanchez2025}. At the same time, Ojonuba et al. reported that a gamified web-development system improved performance but did not produce significant differences in engagement or motivation, showing that positive learning effects do not necessarily appear in every measured outcome \parencite{ojonuba2025webgamification}.

\subsection{Problem Statement}

Although gamification is increasingly adopted in educational settings, current research does not present a completely consistent picture of its effectiveness. Many studies report positive relationships between gamification and student engagement, motivation, achievement, satisfaction, or learning performance \parencite{dahri2025aigamification,wang2024gamification,sevillacampoverde2025elearning,slamet2025}. However, other evidence suggests that the effects depend strongly on how gamification is designed and implemented. For example, Ojonuba et al. found that students using a gamified system achieved better performance, yet engagement and motivation were not significantly different from the non-gamified group \parencite{ojonuba2025webgamification}. Bai et al. similarly observed that students initially engaged with a gamified e-learning design but gradually lost interest until a fantasy element was introduced in a later design cycle \parencite{bai2022fantasy}.

These mixed findings indicate that the central issue is not simply whether gamification works, but \textit{how}, \textit{when}, and \textit{under what conditions} it enhances education and learning. Differences in game elements, learner characteristics, subject domain, educational level, implementation period, technology, and research design may all influence outcomes. Therefore, a critical review is needed to compare recent studies, identify areas of agreement and disagreement, and determine the design principles and limitations that should guide future educational gamification. Furthermore, comprehensive reviews of primary and secondary education indicate that while gamification generally improves motivation and engagement, motivation alone is not always a reliable predictor of academic success \parencite{ruiz2024}

\subsection{Objectives of the Review}

This review article aims to achieve the following objectives:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item To examine the theoretical foundations that explain how gamification influences student motivation, engagement, and learning.
    \item To synthesize recent empirical evidence on the effects of gamification on academic performance, engagement, motivation, satisfaction, and collaborative learning.
    \item To compare different implementations of gamification across higher education, e-learning, vocational education, web development, and AI-supported learning contexts.
    \item To identify common advantages, limitations, and design factors associated with successful or unsuccessful gamification interventions.
    \item To determine research gaps and propose future directions for improving gamification in education and learning.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Research Questions}

This review is guided by the following research questions:

\vspace{0.2cm}
\textbf{RQ1:} What theoretical mechanisms explain how gamification influences student motivation, engagement, and learning outcomes?

\vspace{0.2cm}
\textbf{RQ2:} What does recent empirical evidence reveal about the effectiveness of gamification in different educational contexts?

\vspace{0.2cm}
\textbf{RQ3:} Which gamification design and implementation approaches are associated with positive educational outcomes?

\vspace{0.2cm}
\textbf{RQ4:} What limitations, inconsistencies, and research gaps remain in current educational gamification research?

\section{Literature Review}

This is the \textbf{main section of the review article}.

\subsection{Theoretical Foundations: Self-Determination Theory and Gamification Design}

Gao provides a theoretical examination of gamification through Self-Determination Theory (SDT), arguing that gamification outcomes depend on more than the presence of game elements \parencite{gao2024}. This aligns with broader systemic reviews of gamification design frameworks, which warn that while well-designed gamified environments foster cognitive benefits and intrinsic motivation, an over-reliance on external rewards can ultimately erode that same motivation \parencite{christopoulos2023}. The paper highlights three underexplored SDT ideas: the continuum of motivation, the mutually supportive nature of basic psychological needs, and the functional significance of an event. These ideas suggest that the same reward, challenge, or feedback mechanism can be motivating for one learner but controlling or discouraging for another. Effective gamification should therefore support \textbf{autonomy}, \textbf{competence}, and \textbf{relatedness} rather than rely only on extrinsic rewards \parencite{gao2024}.

This theoretical perspective is reflected in several empirical studies. Ojonuba et al. designed their web-development gamification using SDT and Flow Theory, combining levels, points, badges, leaderboards, and challenges with structured feedback \parencite{ojonuba2025webgamification}. Sevilla-Campoverde et al. similarly discuss SDT and the RAMP dimensions of relatedness, autonomy, mastery, and purpose as explanations for why gamification can increase motivation and make learning activities more meaningful \parencite{sevillacampoverde2025elearning}. Dahri et al. extend the design perspective by examining AI-driven personalization, adaptive feedback, and individualized rewards as mechanisms that may strengthen engagement and academic achievement \parencite{dahri2025aigamification}.

Bai et al. provide further evidence that design details matter. Their first design cycle used a goal-access-feedback-challenge-collaboration model, but student participation declined after the initial sessions. In a second cycle, the researchers added a fantasy element, creating a GAFCC-F model that produced better online interaction, learning experience, and learning performance \parencite{bai2022fantasy}. This finding supports Gao's argument that the motivational meaning of gamification depends on how design elements are experienced by learners rather than on the simple addition of points or rewards \parencite{gao2024}.

\subsection{Empirical Evidence: Meta-Analysis of Gamification in Collaborative Learning}

Slamet and Meng provide the broadest empirical synthesis among the reviewed studies. A recent synthesis of systematic literature reviews further contextualizes these outcomes, revealing that while 56\% of studies report significant positive effects on student motivation, only 33\% report direct improvements in academic performance \parencite{jaramillomediavilla2024}. Their meta-analysis identified 35 relevant studies comparing gamified and non-gamified collaborative learning conditions \parencite{slamet2025}. Twenty-eight studies examined cognitive outcomes involving 3,296 participants, while 22 studies examined attitudinal outcomes involving 2,862 participants. The overall effect was large for cognitive learning outcomes ($d=0.875$) and moderate-to-large for attitudinal outcomes ($d=0.748$), indicating that gamification can substantially enhance both learning performance and learners' attitudes in collaborative settings \parencite{slamet2025}.

The individual empirical studies in this review generally support this positive pattern, but they also reveal important variation. Dahri et al. used a quantitative survey and SEM analysis with Malaysian students and found significant positive relationships between gamification and academic achievement, as well as between gamification and student engagement \parencite{dahri2025aigamification}. Wang et al. evaluated gamified learning software with 71 vocational-college students and reported positive responses for learning involvement, motivation, attractiveness of content, enjoyment, challenge, and willingness to continue using the software \parencite{wang2024gamification}.

Sevilla-Campoverde et al. used a mixed-methods design in an online graduate course. Final grades, a Likert-scale satisfaction survey, and open-ended responses were analyzed using Spearman correlation and content analysis. The study reported an average assessment result of 9.78/10 and a significant positive correlation between satisfaction with gamification and grades ($r=0.673$, $p<0.05$) \parencite{sevillacampoverde2025elearning}.

However, Ojonuba et al. provide an important counterpoint. In a study of 60 university students, the gamified group achieved better performance than the non-gamified group, but the researchers found no significant difference in engagement or motivation \parencite{ojonuba2025webgamification}. This mixed result shows why the reviewed literature should not be interpreted as evidence that gamification automatically improves every educational outcome.

\subsection{Practical Implementation: Gamification Case Study in Higher Education}

Alonso-S\'anchez et al. demonstrate a comprehensive higher-education implementation of gamification in two Educational Sciences courses involving 354 students \parencite{alonsosanchez2025}. The experience incorporated a narrative, points, levels, rankings, challenges, missions, badges, rewards, Kahoot, Quizizz, Educaplay, and a WIX-based platform. Students generally reported positive perceptions of their learning, expected academic outcomes, class participation, and motivation, and they preferred the gamified approach to traditional teaching methods \parencite{alonsosanchez2025}.

Other reviewed studies show how practical implementation can vary by context. Wang et al. developed C\# learning software to teach system reliability concepts in applied mathematics, integrating game rules with mathematical derivation and evaluating the software with 71 students \parencite{wang2024gamification}. Ojonuba et al. implemented a web-based gamified environment for web-development education and compared it with a non-gamified system \parencite{ojonuba2025webgamification}. Sevilla-Campoverde et al. structured an online graduate course using the Six Steps to Gamification framework and combined quantitative and qualitative evaluation \parencite{sevillacampoverde2025elearning}.

Bai et al. are especially relevant to practical design because their two-cycle design-based research demonstrates the importance of refinement. The first design produced declining participation over time; the second design added fantasy and achieved better interaction and learning outcomes \parencite{bai2022fantasy}. Together, these studies suggest that successful implementation requires iterative design, meaningful feedback, appropriate challenge, and alignment between game mechanics and the educational task.

\section{Synthesis of Findings}

\subsection{What Do the Studies Agree On?}

The reviewed studies generally agree that gamification can improve important educational outcomes when it is meaningfully integrated with teaching and learning. Positive outcomes include cognitive learning, academic performance, engagement, motivation, satisfaction, participation, and willingness to continue learning \parencite{slamet2025,alonsosanchez2025,dahri2025aigamification,wang2024gamification,sevillacampoverde2025elearning}. The studies also agree that design quality matters. Clear goals, feedback, appropriate challenge, progression, collaboration, personalization, and meaningful narratives appear more important than simply adding rewards \parencite{gao2024,bai2022fantasy,ojonuba2025webgamification}.

\subsection{What Do the Studies Disagree On?}

The main disagreement concerns the consistency of effects on motivation and engagement. Dahri et al., Wang et al., and Alonso-S\'anchez et al. report positive engagement or motivation-related outcomes \parencite{dahri2025aigamification,wang2024gamification,alonsosanchez2025}. In contrast, Ojonuba et al. found better performance without significant improvements in engagement or motivation \parencite{ojonuba2025webgamification}. Bai et al. also show that initial engagement can decline as learners become familiar with the gamification scheme, although redesign with a fantasy element improved the later experience \parencite{bai2022fantasy}. These differences indicate that gamification effects depend on the design, learners, context, and duration rather than following a universal pattern.

\subsection{Which Approaches Appear to Be Most Effective?}

The strongest evidence supports approaches that combine several characteristics. First, gamification should be theory-informed and support autonomy, competence, and relatedness rather than rely only on controlling rewards \parencite{gao2024}. Second, game elements should be aligned with the learning objective and provide meaningful feedback and appropriately difficult challenges \parencite{ojonuba2025webgamification,sevillacampoverde2025elearning}. Third, collaborative, narrative, or fantasy-based elements can help sustain interaction and reduce the loss of interest associated with novelty effects \parencite{bai2022fantasy,slamet2025}. Finally, adaptive or personalized approaches, including AI-supported personalization, may help address differences between learners \parencite{dahri2025aigamification}.

\subsection{What Trends Can Be Observed?}

Several trends are visible in recent research. There is a shift from simple points-and-badges approaches toward more theoretically grounded and comprehensive designs \parencite{gao2024,alonsosanchez2025}. Researchers are increasingly using stronger research designs, including meta-analysis, mixed methods, quasi-experiments, structural equation modeling, and design-based research \parencite{slamet2025,sevillacampoverde2025elearning,dahri2025aigamification,bai2022fantasy}. Another trend is the growing interest in personalization, adaptive learning, AI-supported gamification, and the use of narratives or fantasy to improve sustained engagement \parencite{dahri2025aigamification,bai2022fantasy}.

\section{Research Gaps}
\label{sec:research-gaps}

Despite significant progress in gamification research, several critical gaps require attention:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Long-Term Effects and Sustainability:} Bai et al. and Ojonuba et al. show that initial interest may decline over time, while several other studies are cross-sectional or short-term \parencite{bai2022fantasy,ojonuba2025webgamification}. More longitudinal evidence is needed.
    \item \textbf{Causal Evidence:} Several studies rely on self-reported perceptions or cross-sectional designs, which limits causal interpretation \parencite{dahri2025aigamification,alonsosanchez2025}.
    \item \textbf{Individual Differences:} Learner expectations, prior gaming experience, learning preferences, and demographic differences may influence outcomes but are not consistently measured \parencite{ojonuba2025webgamification,alonsosanchez2025}.
    \item \textbf{Component-Specific Effects:} Many interventions use several game elements at once, making it difficult to identify which specific element caused an observed effect \parencite{ojonuba2025webgamification}.
    \item \textbf{Context and Generalizability:} Findings often come from a single institution, country, course, or educational level, limiting generalization to other learning environments \parencite{dahri2025aigamification,ojonuba2025webgamification,sevillacampoverde2025elearning}.
    \item \textbf{Teacher Readiness and Resources:} AI-supported and technology-intensive gamification may require infrastructure, training, and resources that are not equally available across institutions \parencite{dahri2025aigamification,alonsosanchez2025}.
    \item \textbf{Measurement Quality:} Some studies rely heavily on perception surveys, while others use objective performance measures; stronger triangulation across behavioral, cognitive, and attitudinal outcomes is needed \parencite{alonsosanchez2025,slamet2025}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Future Research Directions}

Based on the identified research gaps, the following priorities are proposed for future research:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Longitudinal Studies:} Examine whether engagement, motivation, and achievement improvements persist after novelty effects disappear \parencite{bai2022fantasy,ojonuba2025webgamification}.
    \item \textbf{Experimental and Comparative Designs:} Use randomized or stronger quasi-experimental designs to establish causal relationships between gamification and learning outcomes \parencite{dahri2025aigamification,alonsosanchez2025}.
    \item \textbf{Component-Level Testing:} Use A/B testing or factorial designs to isolate the effects of individual game elements such as points, badges, leaderboards, challenges, feedback, narrative, and fantasy \parencite{ojonuba2025webgamification}.
    \item \textbf{Adaptive and Personalized Gamification:} Investigate systems that adapt difficulty, rewards, feedback, and learning paths to learner characteristics and progress \parencite{dahri2025aigamification}.
    \item \textbf{Cross-Cultural and Multi-Institutional Research:} Replicate studies across countries, disciplines, educational levels, and institutions to improve generalizability \parencite{dahri2025aigamification,ojonuba2025webgamification}.
    \item \textbf{Mixed Evidence Sources:} Combine self-report data with academic records, interaction logs, observations, interviews, and teacher reflections \parencite{alonsosanchez2025}.
    \item \textbf{Teacher and Institutional Readiness:} Study the training, infrastructure, accessibility, and implementation support required to scale gamification effectively \parencite{dahri2025aigamification,alonsosanchez2025}.
\end{enumerate}

% =========================================
% 8. DISCUSSION
% =========================================

\section{Discussion}

The reviewed literature shows that gamification has meaningful potential to enhance education and learning, but its effects are conditional rather than automatic. The strongest overall evidence comes from the meta-analysis by Slamet and Meng, which reports substantial positive effects on cognitive and attitudinal learning outcomes in collaborative learning \parencite{slamet2025}. This broad result is supported by individual studies reporting positive relationships with academic achievement, satisfaction, motivation, participation, and continued use \parencite{dahri2025aigamification,wang2024gamification,sevillacampoverde2025elearning,alonsosanchez2025}.

At the same time, the findings from Ojonuba et al. and Bai et al. prevent an overly simple conclusion. Ojonuba et al. found that a gamified system improved performance without significantly improving engagement or motivation \parencite{ojonuba2025webgamification}. Bai et al. observed declining participation after the novelty of the original gamification model faded, but the addition of fantasy improved the second design cycle \parencite{bai2022fantasy}. These findings suggest that gamification should be evaluated not only by whether game elements are present, but by whether they create meaningful learning experiences that remain effective over time.

Gao's theoretical analysis helps explain this variation. According to SDT, the same game element may support or undermine motivation depending on how learners interpret it and whether it supports autonomy, competence, and relatedness \parencite{gao2024}. This suggests that a leaderboard, reward, or challenge cannot be assumed to be motivating in every situation. The practical studies reinforce this point by showing the importance of feedback, learner expectations, meaningful challenge, personalization, narrative, and alignment with course objectives \parencite{bai2022fantasy,ojonuba2025webgamification,dahri2025aigamification}.

Overall, the literature supports gamification as a useful educational strategy, especially when implemented as a coherent instructional design rather than as a collection of superficial rewards. The evidence also indicates that future practice should use student feedback, multiple outcome measures, and iterative refinement to ensure that gamification remains relevant and pedagogically meaningful.

% =========================================
% 9. CONCLUSION
% =========================================

\section{Conclusion}

This review examined how gamification enhances education and learning by integrating theoretical, meta-analytical, design-based, case-study, quantitative, quasi-experimental, software-development, and mixed-methods research. The reviewed evidence generally indicates that gamification can improve cognitive learning, academic performance, engagement, motivation, satisfaction, participation, and learning experience when it is carefully designed and aligned with educational objectives \parencite{slamet2025,dahri2025aigamification,wang2024gamification,sevillacampoverde2025elearning,alonsosanchez2025}.

However, the evidence also shows that gamification is not universally effective across all outcomes. Performance may improve without corresponding increases in engagement or motivation, and initial interest may decrease as novelty fades \parencite{ojonuba2025webgamification,bai2022fantasy}. These findings support Gao's argument that the effectiveness of gamification depends on motivational quality, learner interpretation, and the degree to which design supports autonomy, competence, and relatedness \parencite{gao2024}.

The most promising direction is therefore not simply to increase the number of game elements, but to develop theory-informed, adaptive, meaningful, and context-sensitive gamification. Future research should use longitudinal and stronger experimental designs, test individual game components, examine diverse learner populations, and evaluate implementation requirements across different educational settings. With these improvements, gamification can be used more effectively as a pedagogical strategy for enhancing education and learning.

\end{justify}
