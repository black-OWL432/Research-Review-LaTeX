\vspace{0.5cm}

\noindent
\textbf{Abstract}

\vspace{0.2cm}

\begin{justify}

Educational gamification now produces well-documented engagement and achievement gains alongside equally well-documented harms, including competitive leaderboard anxiety, undermining of intrinsic motivation, and superficial point-chasing, yet most platforms still deploy one fixed configuration to every learner with no mechanism to detect or constrain harm as it occurs. This proposal specifies an adaptive, harm-aware gamification controller that estimates a learner state of engagement and anxiety from platform interaction traces, computes a quantitative harm score against a defined threshold, and selects, retains, or downgrades gamification elements under that constraint, together with a semester-long quasi-experimental evaluation comparing the controller with static gamification and non-gamified baselines. The study is expected to yield a validated real-time learner-state model, an operational harm metric, and a set of element-configuration rules that preserve the moderate-to-large engagement and achievement effects reported in the literature while reducing anxiety and motivational degradation, thereby providing educators with an evidence-based and ethically accountable basis for deploying gamification at scale.

\vspace{0.3cm}

\textbf{Keywords:} Adaptive Gamification; Harm Mitigation; Learner Modelling; Quasi-Experimental Design; Student Engagement

\end{justify}

\section{Introduction}

% Establish the broader CS field (adaptive/intelligent learning systems) and explain
% why adaptive gamification with harm mitigation matters in the current context.
% Include: background, relevance, brief roadmap of the proposal.

The integration of artificial intelligence into education has shifted learning platforms from
static content repositories toward intelligent, adaptive systems that model the learner and
personalise what is presented next \parencite{vistorte2024integrating,naseer2025game}. Within
these systems, gamification -- points, badges, leaderboards, challenges and narratives -- has
become the standard mechanism for sustaining motivation and participation, and meta-analytic
evidence confirms that it produces moderate-to-large benefits for knowledge achievement and
motivation \parencite{lu2024ai}. The counter-evidence is now equally systematic: peer-reviewed
reviews published between 2022 and 2025 document performance pressure and psychological strain,
superficial point-chasing, motivational decline and anxiety \parencite{nugroho2025negative,edwards2022anxiety},
together with compulsive, notification-driven engagement among higher-education students
\parencite{gul2026brainrot}.

The difficulty is that most platforms respond to this evidence by selecting one fixed
configuration and applying it to every learner. Because the same element carries a different
functional significance for different learners \parencite{gao2024sdt}, and because harm emerges
during use rather than only in post-hoc surveys, a static design cannot detect when it is causing
damage and has no mechanism to withdraw or downgrade the element responsible while the learner is
still engaged. Recent prototypes show that runtime adaptation of game elements is technically
feasible \parencite{zourmpakis2023adaptive,shabadurai2025reinforcement}, yet they adapt toward
engagement alone and evaluate benefits only, so the closed loop between harm measurement and
element selection remains open.

This proposal therefore specifies an \textbf{adaptive, harm-aware gamification controller} that
estimates learner state from platform interaction traces, computes a quantitative harm score
against an individualised threshold, and selects, retains or downgrades gamification elements
under that constraint, together with a semester-long quasi-experimental evaluation against
static-gamified and non-gamified baselines. The remainder of the proposal is organised as follows.
Section~2 reviews the literature thematically around the three research questions. Section~3 states
the problem using the context--gap--solution structure, Sections~4 and~5 present the research
questions and objectives, Section~6 details the methodology, Section~7 states the expected
outcomes, and Sections~8 and~9 give the project timeline and conclusion.

\section{Literature Review}

This proposal builds on the group's preceding critical research review of gamification in education, which synthesised recent peer-reviewed studies published between 2022 and 2025 spanning theoretical analysis of motivation, meta-analytic evidence synthesis, systematic documentation of negative effects, and classroom implementation in higher education, artificial intelligence education, web development, and vocational training. Rather than repeating that review article by article, the literature is organised below thematically around the three research questions of this proposal, so that the theoretical foundation, the documented harms, and the available evaluation evidence can be read directly against the design problem this proposal addresses.

\subsection{Adaptive and Personalised Gamification (Link to \rqlink{1})}

Current adaptive and personalized learner frameworks such as the Hexad model,
and artificial intelligence to tailor the content and mechanics according to 
user preferences \parencite{zourmpakis2023adaptive,vistorte2024integrating}. Learner-state
modeling architectures routinely update player profiles by analyzing their 
interactions, ranging from in-app feedback and questionnares to multimodal signals
like EEG data, facial expressions and speech \parencite{zourmpakis2023adaptive,vistorte2024integrating}.
In order to process these signals, systems employ advanced computing techniques and deep 
learning models to predict affective states and cognitive needs \parencite{zourmpakis2023adaptive}
However, while many tutoring systems successfully adjust the content to maximize engagement,
certain game elements such as badges, currency, and forced cooperation have been shown to 
inadverdantly induce significant learner anxiety and stress \parencite{zourmpakis2023adaptive,vistorte2024integrating}.
Consequently, a critifal focus for future research is the developent of robust, closed-loop real-time 
adaptation mechanisms capable of utilizing harm-detection algorithms to lessen the mechanics before 
they negatively impact the user \parencite{zourmpakis2023adaptive,vistorte2024integrating, farhan2023machine}. Experimental
evaluations of these systems typically utilize platforms like Unity or custom web applications,
relying on quasi-experimental pre-/post-test comparisons and continuous engagement logs to measure
the efficacy of dynamic adaptation \parencite{zourmpakis2023adaptive, shabadurai2025reinforcement, naseer2025game}.


\subsection{Documented Negative Effects and Harm-Mitigating Design (Link to \rqlink{2})}

A systematic review published under PRISMA 2020 guidelines screened 206 records across seven databases and retained 20 studies, from which it derived four recurring categories of harm: performance pressure and psychological strain, superficial learning effects, motivational-engagement decline, and psychological distress and emotional strain \parencite{nugroho2025negative}. The same review showed that harm is element-specific and conditional: leaderboards demotivate learners in low ranks, excessive challenges induce frustration, and feedback that lacks direction produces anxiety, whereas harm is minimised when elements align with learning objectives and learner characteristics \parencite{nugroho2025negative}. \textcite{edwards2022anxiety} reached a compatible conclusion from a separate corpus: across 40 studies, anxiety was examined in only 10, and competitive elements failed to reduce anxiety in half of the studies that used them, while quests, narratives and serious games were consistently associated with lower anxiety. From a theoretical standpoint, \textcite{gao2024sdt} argued that self-determination theory (SDT) has been applied superficially in gamification research, and that the same element carries different \textbf{functional significance} for different learners, so fixed configurations cannot be assumed to be uniformly beneficial. Extending the concern beyond motivation, \textcite{gul2026brainrot} documented how reward- and notification-driven design contributes to compulsive digital engagement among 113 higher-education students, manifested as attention deficits, procrastination and distorted time perception. Taken together, the literature documents \textbf{what} harms occur and \textbf{which} elements trigger them, but it does not provide a quantity that a running system can evaluate, nor evidence that the proposed mitigations work when applied dynamically.

\subsection{Evaluation Designs and Evidence for Gamification Effectiveness (Link to \rqlink{3})}

Evaluation of educational gamification is dominated by quasi-experimental and controlled
comparisons, and the reported effects are consistently positive but bounded in three ways that
matter for this proposal. Quasi-experimental comparisons report significant gains: Zhang et al.
compared gamified with traditional instruction over four weeks with 136 university students and
found experimental-group advantages on learning performance, classroom participation, interest,
motivation and self-efficacy \parencite{zhang2026gamification}, while Ojonuba et al. compared
gamified and non-gamified web-development systems with 60 students and obtained higher performance
in the gamified arm but no significant difference in engagement or motivation, a pattern they
attributed partly to novelty effects and individual differences \parencite{ojonuba2025web}.
Longer evaluations exist but remain single-condition: Durmaz et al. varied leaderboard and point
designs over a four-month course with 392 students while holding other elements constant, and
reported lower fear of failure, fewer performance-avoidance goals and better grades in the
redesigned condition \parencite{durmaz2022gamification}. Rigorous designs are available too, as in
the cluster-randomised trial of López-Fernández et al. with 217 computer science students, where
an active-learning activity significantly outperformed a lecture control on knowledge and
motivation \parencite{lopezfernandez2024lego}. At the synthesis level, Lu et al. meta-analysed 22
empirical studies and reported an overall random-effects estimate of Hedges' $g=0.740$, with
$g=0.841$ for knowledge achievement, $g=0.743$ for motivation and $g=0.367$ for thinking skills,
and found that effects shrink as the experimental period lengthens \parencite{lu2024ai}.

Taken together, this evidence base supports the value of gamification but leaves \rqlink{3}
unanswered. Interventions are short and effects decline with duration \parencite{lu2024ai,ming2025gamification};
every study compares one fixed configuration with one control, so adaptation is never the
treatment; and outcomes are measured overwhelmingly as benefits, with anxiety, need frustration
and reward-chasing absent \parencite{edwards2022anxiety}. What is missing is a long-duration,
multi-condition comparison in which the treatment itself adapts and harm is measured on the same
schedule as benefit.

\subsection{Gap Identification}

Three gaps follow from the preceding themes, and they map directly onto the research questions of this proposal. In relation to \rqlink{1}, the literature affirms that learners respond differently to identical elements and repeatedly recommends personalisation, yet it provides no real-time learner-state model and no validated feedback loop between an estimated state and element selection. In relation to \rqlink{2}, harms are well documented and attributable to particular design choices, yet no operational harm metric exists that a system could compute online and enforce as a constraint on deployment. In relation to \rqlink{3}, evidence of effectiveness is substantial but derives from short, non-randomised, single-condition comparisons that measure benefits without commensurate measurement of harms, leaving the central practical question of whether an adaptive, harm-aware configuration preserves attainment gains while reducing documented negative effects unanswered. The proposal addresses these three gaps jointly, because a harm metric is actionable only if learner state can be estimated in real time, and a state model is meaningful only if its output constrains element selection and is subsequently evaluated against both benefit and harm criteria. The problem statement below formalises this transition from context to gap to proposed solution.

\section{Problem Statement}

\subsection{Context}

Existing studies show that the effectiveness of educational gamification has been evaluated using several research designs, with quasi-experimental and controlled comparison approaches being commonly applied. For example, Zhang et al. conducted a quasi-experimental study involving 136 university students and compared gamified instruction with traditional teaching over a four-week intervention. Their evaluation considered not only learning performance, but also classroom participation, learning interest, motivation, and self-efficacy \parencite{zhang2026gamification}. This indicates that the effectiveness of gamification should not be judged based on academic scores alone, as motivational and behavioural outcomes are also important when evaluating the overall learning experience.
A similar comparison was carried out by Ojonuba et al., who evaluated a gamified web-based learning system against a non-gamified system using the same course content. The study assessed engagement, motivation, and performance, and found that the gamified group achieved better learning performance, although the differences in engagement and motivation were not statistically significant \parencite{ojonuba2025web}. Such findings show that gamification may improve certain learning outcomes without necessarily producing the same level of improvement across every measured dimension. Therefore, using a clear baseline and multiple evaluation metrics is important when determining whether a gamified system is genuinely effective.
The duration of the evaluation is another important factor. Durmaz et al. examined the use of gamification during a four-month university course involving 392 students. The study compared different designs of leaderboard and point mechanisms while keeping other elements, such as badges and activities, unchanged. In addition to course performance, the researchers examined motivational factors such as fear of failure and achievement-goal orientation \parencite{durmaz2022gamification}. This provides evidence that longer evaluations can capture psychological and motivational effects that may not be visible in short-term experiments.
Evidence from broader game-based educational research also suggests that the measured effectiveness can vary according to the type of outcome. Lu et al. analysed 22 empirical studies and reported an overall random-effects estimate of Hedges' $g=0.740$. The reported effects differed across learning outcomes, with $g=0.841$ for knowledge achievement, $g=0.743$ for motivation, and $g=0.367$ for thinking skills \parencite{lu2024ai}. These differences further suggest that a single performance measure is insufficient for evaluating the overall effectiveness of a gamified learning intervention.
However, many evaluations are still relatively short. Ming and Choo examined gamification activities conducted over approximately three teaching weeks and measured engagement through YouTube learning analytics together with questionnaire-based measures of motivation and confidence. The authors explicitly identified the lack of longitudinal assessment as a limitation and suggested that future studies should investigate the long-term effects of gamification on student engagement and learning outcomes \parencite{ming2025gamification}. Taken together, the existing literature demonstrates that controlled comparisons, multidimensional outcome measures, and effect-size analysis are useful for evaluating gamification, but it also shows the need for longer-term evaluation that considers both learning benefits and possible motivational or psychological effects.

Gamification has become a standard feature of digital learning: platforms such as Kahoot! and Quizizz routinely deploy points, challenges and leaderboards to raise motivation and participation, creating an implicit expectation that gamification is universally beneficial \parencite{nugroho2025negative}. The benefit evidence is real but not uniform, and the counter-evidence is now systematic rather than anecdotal. \textcite{nugroho2025negative} classified the negative effects of ten common game elements into four recurring categories -- performance pressure and psychological strain, superficial learning effects, motivational-engagement decline, and psychological distress and emotional strain -- and reported that the effectiveness of points was experienced unevenly: 10.1\% of respondents judged points not at all helpful, while only 13.1\% judged them beneficial. Independent evidence converges on the same pattern: in a qualitative systematic review of 40 gamification studies, \textcite{edwards2022anxiety} found that anxiety -- a central learner affect -- was examined in only 10 studies, and that challenge- and competition-based elements failed to reduce anxiety in 50\% of the studies employing them. \textcite{gul2026brainrot} extended this concern beyond motivation to compulsive, notification-driven engagement among 113 higher-education students, and recommended usage regulation and screen-time monitoring as preventive measures. SDT, the dominant explanatory framework for gamification, has been applied superficially, so the mechanisms by which a given element helps or harms a given learner remain under-specified \parencite{gao2024sdt}.

Across digital education and corporate training environments, traditional instructional methods consistently struggle with high 
attrition, user isolation, and a lack ofsustained motivation \parencite{naseer2025game,shabadurai2025reinforcement}. While gamification
has emerged as a popular motivational strategy to combat this, conventional "one-size-fits-all" models rely on static, predefined
rule systems and uniform reward structures that fail to adapt to evolving individual preferences or behaviours, ultimately losing user
interest over time \parencite{farhan2023machine,shabadurai2025reinforcement,zourmpakis2023adaptive}. Furthermore, because effective
learning and engagement are deeply tied to dynamic emotional and cognitive states, rigid platforms often fail to appropriately 
address user frustration or disengagement as it occurs \parencite{naseer2025game,vistorte2024integrating}. Although recent advancements
in artificial intelligence, machine learning, and affective computing offer the capability to assess user emotions in real-time
and personalize game mechanics, these integrated adaptive systems remain underexplored and introduce complex challenges regarding 
data privacy, algorithmic bias, and accurate behavior prediction \parencite{farhan2023machine,vistorte2024integrating}. Consequently,
there is a critical need to develop and validate robust, AI-driven adaptive gamification frameworks that can dynamically tailor both 
instructional content and motivational strategies to individual users while successfully navigating these ethical and technical constraints
\parencite{farhan2023machine,naseer2025game,shabadurai2025reinforcement}.



\subsection{Gap}

Three shortcomings follow from this evidence. First, \textbf{harms are documented as qualitative categories and post-hoc associations, not as an operational quantity}: no study produces a numeric harm metric that a running system can evaluate at decision time. Second, \textbf{the mitigations that have been proposed are single-shot design recommendations}: relative leaderboards that expose only neighbouring ranks \parencite{nugroho2025negative}, cooperative rather than competitive structures \parencite{edwards2022anxiety}, a gradually reduced dose of gamification as autonomous motivation develops \parencite{gao2024sdt}, and usage regulation or digital detox \parencite{gul2026brainrot} are each evaluated under static conditions, and none is deployed as a closed-loop response to measured learner state. \textcite{nugroho2025negative} further noted that the combined effect of elements applied together remains unexamined. Third, \textbf{measurement is unbalanced and insensitive to individual differences}: engagement and achievement are recorded continuously, whereas anxiety, need frustration and reward-chasing are measured rarely or not at all \parencite{edwards2022anxiety}, even though the functional significance of an identical element differs across learners \parencite{gao2024sdt}. Consequently, a platform cannot currently answer the question that \rqlink{2} poses: which configuration minimises documented harm while preserving engagement.

Despite the growing implementation of adaptive gamification, current approaches to modelling learner states remain largely constrained 
by static user typologies and rigid, rule-based decision systems that fail to capture the fluid and multidimensional nature 
of student motivation and emotion \parencite{shabadurai2025reinforcement,zourmpakis2023adaptive}. Most existing platforms 
rely heavily on basic behavioral metrics—such as quiz attempts, completion rates, or time-on-task—while demonstrating a 
limited focus on integrating real-time affective computing to continuously detect transient emotional states like anxiety, 
frustration, or confusion \parencite{naseer2025game}. Furthermore, while some frameworks utilize machine learning to predict 
engagement, there is a critical gap in seamlessly assessing real-time emotional fluctuations without relying on complex, 
advanced sensing tools such as EEG or continuous facial recognition that pose significant privacy, accuracy, and implementation 
challenges in everyday online learning environments \parencite{farhan2023machine,vistorte2024integrating}. Consequently, 
contemporary systems lack the sophisticated, real-time emotional state modelling required to dynamically select and calibrate 
specific gamification elements in a way that simultaneously sustains deep engagement and mitigates performance-induced anxiety 
\parencite{naseer2025game,zourmpakis2023adaptive}.

A further gap concerns how the effectiveness of gamification is evaluated over time. Although controlled and quasi-experimental comparisons are increasingly used, many studies still rely on relatively short interventions and immediate post-intervention measurements. For example, Zhang et al. evaluated gamified instruction over only four weeks and acknowledged that immediate post-intervention measures may be affected by novelty and social-desirability effects, while the short duration prevents observation of longer-term changes in motivation and engagement \parencite{zhang2026gamification}. Similarly, Ming and Choo examined gamification activities across approximately three teaching weeks and explicitly noted that longitudinal effects were not assessed \parencite{ming2025gamification}. Ojonuba et al. also highlighted methodological weaknesses in existing studies, including short treatment periods, limited comparison groups, single assessments, and insufficient use of validated measures \parencite{ojonuba2025web}.
Longer evaluations do exist; for instance, Durmaz et al. conducted a four-month study and measured both academic performance and psychological variables such as fear of failure \parencite{durmaz2022gamification}. However, such studies mainly evaluate fixed gamification designs rather than systems that dynamically adapt game elements in response to possible harmful learner states. Therefore, the reviewed evidence still leaves an evaluation gap: there is limited evidence from long-duration studies that compare an adaptive, harm-aware gamification approach with both static gamification and non-gamified baselines while measuring learning benefits and potential psychological harms together. This makes it difficult to determine whether improvements in performance or engagement can be sustained without increasing negative motivational or emotional effects over a full semester.

\subsection{Solution}

We propose an \textbf{adaptive, harm-aware gamification controller} that closes this loop. The system estimates each learner's state from platform interaction logs and brief self-report, computes a quantitative harm score $H_t$ that aggregates anxiety, need-frustration, superficial-learning, compulsive-use and social-comparison indicators, and constrains element selection so that a configuration is applied only while $H_t \le \tau$, where $\tau$ is calibrated to the individual learner's own baseline. When the constraint is violated, competitive and controlling elements are substituted or downgraded according to the rules specified in Table~\ref{tab:downgrade-rules}, and gamification intensity is tapered as autonomous motivation increases \parencite{gao2024sdt}. The controller is evaluated in a semester-long quasi-experimental design against static-gamified and non-gamified baselines using balanced benefit-and-harm metrics. This directly addresses \rqlink{2}, supplies the harm constraint required by \rqlink{1}, and provides the treatment condition for \rqlink{3}.

Building a robust learner-state model requires a dynamic, multi-layered architecture that continuously captures and processes
a student's cognitive and emotional data in real time. The process typically begins with a baseline initialization, where
psychological assessments like the Hexad framework are used to categorize the user's inherent motivational drivers and
gamification preferences \parencite{shabadurai2025reinforcement,zourmpakis2023adaptive}. Once active learning commences, the model
continuously ingests multimodal data, logging behavioral telemetry such as time-on-task and login frequency alongside
cognitive performance metrics like quiz scores and mastery levels \parencite{naseer2025game}. Crucially, this behavioral
and cognitive data is fused with affective signals—captured through self-reported feedback, in-app dialogues, or 
advanced affective computing technologies like facial expression analysis and biosignals—to continuously monitor transient 
emotions such as anxiety, frustration, or engagement \parencite{naseer2025game,vistorte2024integrating}. Machine learning algorithms 
then process this aggregated data to refine the profile in real time, utilizing techniques such as Bayesian knowledge tracing 
to estimate academic success, supervised learning to predict disengagement, and weighted averages to dynamically update the 
learner's motivational trait vector \parencite{farhan2023machine,naseer2025game,shabadurai2025reinforcement}. Ultimately, this 
continuously evolving model functions as a central intelligence hub, empowering the system's adaptive engine to deploy precisely 
timed academic interventions and personalized gamification rewards that sustain user motivation and mitigate frustration 
\parencite{farhan2023machine,naseer2025game}

The proposed controller will be evaluated using a 14-week, three-group quasi-experimental longitudinal design. The first group will use the proposed adaptive, harm-aware gamification system, in which game elements are dynamically adjusted according to each learner's current state and harm score. The second group will use static gamification, where the same points, badges, leaderboards, challenges, and feedback rules remain fixed throughout the semester. The third group will act as a non-gamified baseline and will receive the same learning content and assessments without game elements. The learning materials, assessment difficulty, and instructional schedule will be kept consistent across all three conditions so that differences in outcomes can be more reasonably attributed to the gamification strategy. This three-condition structure extends the adaptive-versus-static comparison used in previous adaptive gamification research while also incorporating the non-gamified baseline used in educational gamification studies \parencite{shabadurai2025reinforcement,ojonuba2025web}.
Evaluation will take place at multiple points rather than only after the intervention. Before the semester begins, students will complete a knowledge pre-test, a baseline motivation assessment, and a baseline anxiety assessment. Learning and behavioural data will then be collected continuously throughout the 14 weeks. Platform logs will record indicators such as login frequency, time-on-task, average session length, interaction frequency, activity-completion rate, quiz performance, and module completion. These indicators are consistent with engagement measures previously used to evaluate dynamic adaptive gamification systems \parencite{shabadurai2025reinforcement}. A midpoint assessment will be conducted around Week 7, followed by the final post-test and psychological assessment in Week 14. Repeated measurement is important because previous short-term gamification studies have acknowledged that immediate post-intervention results may be affected by novelty effects and may not represent sustained changes in learner motivation or engagement \parencite{zhang2026gamification,ming2025gamification}.
The evaluation will balance educational benefits with possible psychological harms. Learning effectiveness will be measured using pre-test and post-test performance, weekly quiz results, assessment marks, and completion rates, while engagement will be measured from the behavioural logs. Motivational quality will be assessed using a validated motivation questionnaire, while state anxiety will be measured using the six-item short form of the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI-6), which provides a brief measure suitable for repeated assessment \parencite{marteau1992stai}. Additional harm indicators defined by the proposed harm score $H_t$, including fear of failure, motivational undermining, superficial engagement, compulsive use, and social comparison, will also be monitored. This is necessary because existing evidence shows that competitive elements such as leaderboards and excessive challenges may improve participation for some learners while simultaneously producing anxiety, pressure, frustration, or reduced intrinsic motivation for others \parencite{nugroho2025negative,edwards2022anxiety,durmaz2022gamification}.
For the statistical analysis, changes across the three conditions and across time will be analysed using mixed-effects models, with group, time, and the group-by-time interaction treated as the main effects. This approach allows repeated observations from the same learner to be considered and also reduces the limitations associated with analysing intact classroom groups as if every observation were independent. In addition to statistical significance, effect sizes and 95\% confidence intervals will be reported because the practical magnitude of an intervention is important when interpreting gamification effectiveness. Meta-analytic evidence has shown that effects can differ considerably across outcomes, with stronger effects reported for knowledge achievement and motivation than for higher-order thinking outcomes \parencite{lu2024ai}.
The adaptive condition will be considered successful only if it produces sustained learning and engagement outcomes while reducing the harm indicators relative to static gamification. It will therefore be compared with the static-gamification group to determine whether adaptation reduces anxiety and other negative effects without sacrificing learning benefits, and with the non-gamified group to determine whether the gamification intervention provides meaningful educational gains beyond ordinary learning activities. This balanced evaluation directly addresses the central question of whether adaptive, harm-aware gamification can preserve the benefits of gamification over a full semester while reducing the psychological costs associated with fixed game-element configurations.

\section{Research Questions}

\vspace{0.2cm}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \label{rq:rq1} How can learner state (engagement and anxiety) be modelled in real time to drive adaptive selection of gamification elements in an online learning platform?
    \item \label{rq:rq2} Which gamification design configurations minimise documented negative effects -- anxiety, motivational undermining, and superficial learning -- while preserving the engagement and achievement gains reported in the literature?
    \item \label{rq:rq3} Does an adaptive, harm-aware gamification system improve learning outcomes and motivational quality compared with static gamification and non-gamified baselines over a full semester?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Research Objectives}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{RO1 (links to \rqlink{1}):} Design and evaluate a Dynamic Adaptive Gamification (DAG) 
    architecture over an eight-week implementation period, utilizing effective computing and deep learning 
    models to predict learners' emotional states via multimodal inputs like facial expressions or EEG signals.
    The AI-driven system will continuously update player profiles based on frameworks like the Hexad user typology.
    When frustration or anxiety is detected, the system will dynamically adjust or disable specific 
    stress-inducing game mechanics, such as forced cooperation, time-restricted challenges, or rigid badge collection.
    the intervention's success will be quantified by significant improvements in pre- and post-test knowledge scores,
    increased intrinsic motivation and self-efficacy as measured by the Motivated Strategies for Learning questionnares 
    (MSLQ), and elevated engagement metrics including average session length, time-on-task, and module completion rates.
    \item \textbf{RO2 (links to \rqlink{2}):} Define and validate a quantitative harm metric together with a
    pre-registered set of element-configuration rules that constrain gamification deployment. Specifically,
    operationalise five harm constructs -- anxiety, motivational undermining, superficial learning, compulsive
    engagement, and social comparison -- as platform-log and self-report variables, combine them into an
    individualised harm score $H_t$ with threshold $\tau$, and specify the downgrade rules applied whenever
    $H_t > \tau$. The objective is achieved when the metric has passed the three validation checks of
    Section~\ref{sec:harm} and, over one 14-week semester, the adaptive condition records a weekly mean harm score at
    least 20\% lower than static gamification while weekly active sessions and assessment marks remain within
    a 10\% non-inferiority margin of the static-gamified condition.
    \item \textbf{RO3 (links to \rqlink{3}):} Conduct a 14-week, three-arm quasi-experimental
    evaluation -- adaptive harm-aware gamification, static gamification, and non-gamified
    instruction -- with approximately 150 undergraduates in a single course, using identical
    content, instructors and assessments across arms. The objective is achieved when the adaptive
    condition records a weekly mean harm score at least 20\% lower than static gamification,
    assessment marks and weekly active sessions remain within a 10\% non-inferiority margin of the
    static-gamified condition, both gamified conditions exceed the non-gamified control on
    pre-to-post knowledge gain and engagement, and every effect is reported as Hedges' $g$ with a
    95\% confidence interval by the end of the semester.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Research Methodology}

\subsection{Research Design and Approach}

% Choose: Experimental, Quasi-experimental, Empirical, or Comparative -- with justification.
% Include a pipeline overview (conceptual flow of data -> processing -> output).

The study adopts a quasi-experimental, nonequivalent control-group design with three arms
observed over one 14-week semester: (A) adaptive harm-aware gamification, (S) static gamification
using a single fixed element configuration, and (C) non-gamified instruction. A quasi-experimental
design is chosen for three reasons. Random assignment cannot be imposed on timetable-scheduled
classes, so learners are analysed by their existing class sections, which is the standard practice
in the evaluation literature \parencite{zhang2026gamification,ojonuba2025web}. The intervention
must also be evaluated where it is intended to operate, inside a running course, because the
controller depends on the authentic interaction stream that a laboratory setting would change
\parencite{durmaz2022gamification}. Finally, a pre-intervention baseline week (Week~0) in which
logging is active but no elements are deployed gives each learner a within-subject control for
both the state model and the harm threshold, which partially compensates for the absence of
randomisation. The design is quantitative, longitudinal and confirmatory: the analysis plan, the
downgrade rules and the primary hypothesis are pre-registered before data collection.

The conceptual pipeline is described here in text for LaTeX compatibility. Interaction events
emitted by the learning platform (logins, attempts, time-on-task, reward-screen views, leaderboard
exposures) are captured as xAPI statements and written to an append-only event store. The
state-estimation module converts each learner's current window into an engagement--anxiety
estimate; the harm module combines that estimate with logged and self-reported indicators into
$H_t$, which is compared with $\tau$; the policy module then returns either the adaptive element
configuration or the downgrade configuration specified in Table~\ref{tab:downgrade-rules}. The
selected configuration is rendered by the platform, learner outcomes are logged, and those data
feed both the online update step of Algorithm~\ref{alg:controller} and the offline evaluation
described below. In short, \emph{data $\rightarrow$ state estimation $\rightarrow$ harm scoring
$\rightarrow$ constrained element selection $\rightarrow$ delivery $\rightarrow$ outcome logging}.

\subsection{Technical Approach}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Adaptive harm-aware gamification controller (pseudocode outline)}
\label{alg:controller}
\begin{flushleft}
\fontsize{10pt}{12pt}\selectfont
\textbf{Input:} learner interaction stream $\mathbf{x}_t$, harm threshold $\tau$ \\
\textbf{Output:} gamification element configuration $e_t$
\end{flushleft}
\hrulefill
\vspace{0.2cm}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Estimate learner state $\hat{s}_t = f_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t)$   % engagement + anxiety model
    \item Compute harm score $h(\hat{s}_t, e_{t-1})$
    \item \textbf{if} $h > \tau$ \textbf{then} $e_t \leftarrow$ downgrade competitive elements
    \item \textbf{else} $e_t \leftarrow \pi_\theta(\hat{s}_t)$   % adaptive element selection policy
    \item Observe outcome; update $\theta$   % optimisation / learning step
\end{enumerate}
\hrulefill
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Data and Tools}

\textbf{Data provenance.} This proposal reports no experimental results: the controller, the harm
metric and the evaluation plan are specified here, and the data that will test them do not yet
exist. The \emph{primary} dataset will be collected from the instrumented course platform during
the study, with a planned sample of approximately 150 undergraduates in the single course section
where the three conditions run, observed for 14 weeks plus the Week-0 baseline, with three
self-report waves per learner and continuous event logging; enrolment and event counts are design
targets that will be reported as observed once collection begins. Platform-collected data are
preferred to a public benchmark because the variables required by Equation~\ref{eq:harm} are
event-level and context-specific -- reward-screen dwell time, abandonment within 60 seconds of a
leaderboard view, night-time activity -- and no public repository records these events under
consent for harm analysis. Instruments are reused rather than invented: state anxiety is measured
with the six-item short-form STAI \parencite{marteau1992stai}, and need frustration and intrinsic
motivation with the BPNSFS and IMI identified by \textcite{gao2024sdt} as validated but underused.

\textbf{Replicable dry run on synthetic traces.} Before any human data are collected, the
controller's logic is checked on a synthetic event trace produced by a documented generator:
20 simulated learners over 30 days, one fixed random seed, and all generator parameters (baseline
means and standard deviations, a scheduled anxiety spike on day~12) written down in the script.
The check is intentionally small so that a reader can reproduce it with a single command,
\texttt{python dryrun.py --seed 42}, on an ordinary laptop and assert three properties:
(i)~every configuration actually applied satisfies $H_t \le \tau$; (ii)~the scheduled spike raises
$H_t$ above $\tau$ and triggers the downgrade rules of Table~\ref{tab:downgrade-rules} within one
decision step; and (iii)~a flat trace with no spike produces no downgrade at all. The harness is
specified as follows.

\textbf{Platform instrumentation.} The system is implemented as a plugin for a mainstream LMS
(Moodle 4.x) backed by a Python service. Every gamification element is wrapped by an event emitter
so that exposure, response and abandonment are separable, and every controller decision is written
to an append-only log containing the state estimate, the harm score and the configuration applied,
which makes the controller auditable as required by the ethical specification below.

\textbf{Tools.} The dry run needs only Python 3.11, NumPy and pytest, so the harness of
Algorithm~\ref{alg:dryrun} is replicable without special hardware or licensed software. For the
study itself, PyTorch is used for the state-estimation model, scikit-learn for the comparator
baselines, and pandas for the event pipeline; the models are deliberately small -- gradient-boosted
trees and a shallow multilayer perceptron -- because inference runs per decision window on a single
CPU server rather than on a GPU cluster, which keeps latency low and replication feasible.
Mixed-effects analysis is performed in R with lme4 and in Python with statsmodels; the LaTeX source,
the generator script and the pre-registration are versioned in Git. Hardware is a university-hosted
server (8 cores, 32\,GB RAM); no participant data leave the institution's access-controlled network.

\subsubsection{Ethical Considerations}

Because the study collects behavioural and affect data from human participants, it will be submitted to the university's research ethics committee for approval before recruitment, and data collection will not begin until approval is granted. Participation is voluntary: informed consent is obtained in writing, participants may withdraw at any time without academic penalty, and withdrawal triggers deletion of that participant's records. Learners assigned to the non-gamified control receive identical learning content, assessment and instructor contact, so that no participant is academically disadvantaged by group allocation, and gamification points are excluded from course grading entirely.

Data are minimised, pseudonymised and stored on encrypted, access-controlled systems, with the linkage key held separately by the research team, and processing conforms to the Malaysian Personal Data Protection Act 2010 and to the institution's research-data policy. Raw interaction logs and self-report records are retained for three years from the end of the study and are then securely destroyed, while only de-identified aggregate tables are kept beyond that point. Affect and engagement records are treated as personal data: they are reported only in aggregate, are never shared with instructors on an identifiable basis, and are never used to infer clinical or mental-health status. Consistent with the finding that public ranking removes a learner's choice to disclose performance \parencite{edwards2022anxiety}, leaderboard visibility is opt-in, and the default display is the relative or cooperative form.

Non-maleficence is enforced structurally rather than declared as an intention: the harm threshold $\tau$ functions as a hard constraint inside the controller, so an intervention that would raise harm cannot be selected, and no streak-loss, negative-notification or similar coercive mechanism is implemented, in line with the concerns about compulsive engagement raised by \textcite{gul2026brainrot}. Bias mitigation operates at three points. Individualised thresholds and monitoring of harm rates across demographic subgroups address response bias arising from cultural and individual variation in reactions to competition and rewards, which remains under-examined \parencite{gao2024sdt,nugroho2025negative}; if a subgroup shows a systematically higher harm rate, the affected downgrade rules are reviewed with that subgroup's data held out from rule tuning. Because participants opt in to leaderboard visibility, exposure is not random, so leaderboard analyses are reported both with and without the opt-in indicator as a covariate. Finally, all controller decisions are logged and auditable, the downgrade rules are pre-registered, and the analysis plan is specified before data collection to mitigate both algorithmic and researcher bias.

\subsection{Evaluation Criteria}

\textbf{Model criteria.} Disengagement prediction is evaluated as binary classification
(disengaged during the next session: yes/no) using area under the ROC curve, precision, recall and
F1, with a pre-set acceptance threshold of AUC $\ge 0.75$. The anxiety estimate is evaluated by its
correlation with the concurrent STAI-6 wave at weeks 6 and 12, requiring $r \ge 0.40$. Both are
compared against simple baselines: a session-length-only logistic regression and last-value
persistence.

\textbf{Metric criteria.} The harm score must pass the three validation checks specified in
Section~\ref{sec:harm} -- Cronbach's $\alpha \ge 0.70$ for each multi-item scale, a positive
moderate correlation with the concurrent self-report wave, and prediction of session abandonment
within 24 hours above the no-skill rate -- before it is interpreted as a measure.

\textbf{Outcome criteria.} Educational benefit is measured by assessment marks and pre-to-post
knowledge gain (Hedges' $g$); engagement by weekly active sessions, time-on-task and module
completion rate; motivational quality by the MSLQ and the intrinsic-motivation items; harm by the
weekly mean and weekly peak of $H_t$, the proportion of decision points at which $H_t>\tau$, and the
STAI-6 waves. The primary hypothesis is that condition A records a weekly mean harm score at least
20\% lower than condition S while marks and weekly sessions remain within a 10\% non-inferiority
margin of condition S, and that both gamified conditions exceed condition C on knowledge gain.

\textbf{Baselines and ablation.} Condition S is the static baseline: one literature-standard
configuration applied uniformly to every learner. Condition C is the non-gamified baseline:
identical content, assessments and instructor contact without any game element. Two component
ablations are run offline by replaying logged windows: (i) the policy with the harm constraint
removed, that is, engagement-only adaptation, which isolates the contribution of $H_t$; and (ii) a
rule-based state estimator in place of the learned model, which isolates the contribution of
learning. Because both ablations are evaluated by replay, no participant is exposed to a
configuration that the constraint would have forbidden.

\textbf{Experimental setup and analysis.} All three conditions share content, instructor,
assessment schedule and platform; gamification contributes no course marks. Analyses use linear
mixed-effects models with random intercepts for learner and week, report effect sizes with 95\%
confidence intervals, apply the non-inferiority test at $\alpha = 0.05$, and correct for multiple
comparisons across secondary outcomes. System-level criteria are decision latency below one second
per decision window and complete coverage of active learners by the state model.

\subsection{Harm-Detection Variables and Measurement}
\label{sec:harm}

The harm dimension is operationalised through five constructs drawn from the documented negative effects in the literature, each mapped to at least one behavioural variable computed from platform logs; anxiety and motivational undermining additionally carry validated multi-item self-report instruments, while the remaining constructs use brief single-item prompts, so that every construct is triangulated at least once during the study. Multi-item self-report is administered only three times to limit response burden, single-item prompts take under ten seconds and are event-triggered or weekly, and log-derived variables are used precisely because self-reported usage is known to be biased \parencite{gul2026brainrot}. All variables are oriented before analysis so that a higher value always indicates greater harm: items whose raw direction is opposite (for example, the intrinsic-motivation score, where a lower value indicates greater undermining) are reverse-coded. Table~\ref{tab:harm-constructs} summarises each construct, its operational variables, instruments and sampling cadence.

The design begins with a one-week pre-intervention calibration phase (Week~0), during which logging is active but no gamification elements are deployed. Every variable is standardised against the statistics recorded in this phase, and the first self-report wave is administered at its end, so that each learner's baseline exists before any treatment is applied.

\textbf{Harm score and constraint.} For learner $i$ at decision time $t$, each variable is reverse-coded where necessary so that a higher value always indicates greater harm, then standardised against that learner's own Week-0 baseline, $z(x_{k,t}^{(i)}) = (x_{k,t}^{(i)} - \mu_k^{(i)}) / \sigma_k^{(i)}$. The standardised variables are combined as
\begin{equation}
H_t^{(i)} = \sum_{k=1}^{5} w_k \, z\!\left(x_{k,t}^{(i)}\right), \qquad \text{subject to } H_t^{(i)} \le \tau^{(i)},
\label{eq:harm}
\end{equation}
where $H_t^{(i)}$ is dimensionless and expressed in units of the learner's own baseline variability, so that $H_t > 0$ denotes a state worse than that learner's pre-intervention baseline.

The weights are pre-registered: the primary analysis uses equal weights ($w_k = 1/5$ for all $k$), and any alternative weighting is reported only as a pre-specified sensitivity analysis, so that the metric cannot be tuned after seeing the outcome data. The threshold $\tau^{(i)}$ is computed once per learner during the Week-0 calibration phase as the baseline mean of $H$ plus one baseline standard deviation, $\tau^{(i)} = \mu_{H_0^{(i)}} + \sigma_{H_0^{(i)}}$, and is then held fixed for the semester. Because it is derived from each learner's own baseline distribution rather than a cohort mean, it implements the position that the functional significance of an element differs across learners \parencite{gao2024sdt}. Log-derived variables enter the score daily, whereas self-report waves (Weeks 0, 6 and 12) are carried forward and linearly interpolated between waves; this interpolation is reported as a limitation of the metric. The harm score is reported as a weekly mean together with the weekly peak.

\textbf{Validation of the metric.} $H_t$ is treated as a measurement only if it behaves as one, so three checks are specified before the main analysis: (a) internal consistency of each multi-item self-report scale is reported as Cronbach's $\alpha$ at every wave; (b) the weekly mean of $H_t$ is correlated with the concurrent self-report wave, with a positive moderate association expected; and (c) episodes in which $H_t > \tau$ are tested for their ability to predict session abandonment within the following 24 hours. A failure on these checks leads to re-specification of the affected variable before data collection ends, never to post-hoc re-weighting.

Two design decisions follow from the literature. First, the downgrade rules are fixed before the study begins rather than adjusted during data collection, which limits researcher degrees of freedom and the associated interpretation bias that \textcite{gul2026brainrot} identified as a limitation of qualitative designs. Second, gamification is treated as a scaffold whose intensity is reduced over the semester as autonomous motivation develops, since the gradual withdrawal of gamification has been identified as an urgent research direction \parencite{gao2024sdt}.

\section{Expected Outcomes}

% Anticipated findings, significance to the field, deliverables.
% Must be specific and measurable.

The proposal is expected to yield four specific and measurable contributions.

\textbf{(1) A validated real-time learner-state model.} Engagement and anxiety are estimated from
ordinary platform interaction traces, with a target of AUC $\ge 0.75$ for disengagement prediction
and $r \ge 0.40$ with self-reported state anxiety, without EEG or continuous facial capture, so
that the model is deployable under the privacy constraints of a real course.

\textbf{(2) An operational harm metric.} Equation~\ref{eq:harm} delivers a harm quantity that a
running system can evaluate at decision time rather than a post-hoc qualitative category
\parencite{nugroho2025negative,edwards2022anxiety}, complete with an individualised calibration
procedure for $\tau$, three psychometric and predictive validation checks, and a pre-registered
weighting scheme.

\textbf{(3) A tested set of element-configuration rules.} Table~\ref{tab:downgrade-rules} is
expected to be confirmed or revised empirically, yielding evidence on whether harm can be reduced
by at least 20\% relative to static gamification while marks and weekly sessions stay within a 10\%
non-inferiority margin, and identifying which substitutions (relative leaderboards, reward caps,
difficulty adaptation, narrative prioritisation) carry that effect.

\textbf{(4) A semester-long three-arm comparison.} The evaluation responds directly to the
short-duration, benefit-only designs summarised in Section~2.3 \parencite{lu2024ai,ming2025gamification}
by measuring benefit and harm on the same weekly schedule over 14 weeks.

Deliverables are the instrumented LMS plugin and controller source code, the pre-registered
analysis plan, a de-identified dataset with a data dictionary subject to the retention policy of
Section~6.3, a reproducibility package, and at least one manuscript submitted to a peer-reviewed
venue. For the field, the contribution is a shift from asking \emph{whether} gamification works to
asking \emph{which configuration is safe for which learner at which moment} -- a prerequisite for
deploying gamification responsibly at scale.

\section{Conclusion}

\subsection{Summary of Contributions}

This proposal responds to a gap the literature has made explicit: the benefits of gamification are
well documented and its harms are increasingly well documented, yet no existing system measures
harm as a quantity, reacts to it while the learner is still engaged, or is evaluated against
benefit and harm together. The proposed adaptive, harm-aware controller closes that loop by
combining a real-time engagement--anxiety state model, an individualised harm score $H_t$
constrained by the threshold $\tau$, and a pre-registered set of downgrade rules, all evaluated in
a 14-week three-arm quasi-experiment against static-gamified and non-gamified baselines. The study
is expected to produce a validated state model (AUC $\ge 0.75$), a validated harm metric, and
element-configuration rules, with the central anticipated result that harm falls by at least 20\%
relative to static gamification while marks and weekly sessions remain within a 10\%
non-inferiority margin. Together these answer \rqlink{1} through \rqlink{3} and give educators an
evidence-based and auditable basis for deploying gamification.

\subsection{Final Remarks}

Gamification does not need to be abandoned; it needs to be governed. Treating documented harms as
measurable, constrainable quantities rather than as unavoidable side effects converts the ethical
principle of non-maleficence into an engineering requirement, and gives institutions a defensible
basis for using adaptive gamification in personalised learning at scale. Harm-aware adaptation is
therefore the responsible next step for technology-enhanced learning: the question is no longer
whether game elements can motivate learners, but whether a system can detect when it is doing harm
and act on that knowledge before the learner disengages.


\end{justify}

Zourmpakis et al. &
Imran &
2023 &
Semi-experimental simulations and surveys. &
80 students in Greece &
92.5\% enjoyed the adaptive app. Badges and stories motivated most, but forced cooperation and currency induced stress. &
Dynamic runtime adaptation; strong pedagogical integration. &
Small sample; no control group; hardware limits forced peer grouping. &
Longitudinal studies and comparisons against static gamification.
\tabularnewline
\hline


Farhan et al. &
Imran &
2023 &
Qualitative literature synthesis and conceptual ML gamification framework. &
Secondary literature across various industries. &
ML behavior prediction and reinforcement learning (RL) optimize rewards and prevent user disengagement. &
Cross-disciplinary focus; addresses data ethics and bias. &
Purely theoretical without empirical validation. &
Empirical testing in active platforms; bias-auditing frameworks.
\tabularnewline
\hline


Vistorte et al. &
Imran &
2024 &
PRISMA systematic literature review and bibliometric mapping. &
41 studies on AI educational emotion assessment. &
Deep learning achieves high multimodal emotion recognition accuracy, positively correlating with academic performance. &
Rigorous screening protocol and multimodal scope. &
Publication / language bias; relies on cross-sectional data. &
Closed-loop tutoring integration, federated learning for privacy, and longitudinal validity.
\tabularnewline
\hline


Shabadurai et al. &
Imran &
2025 &
Controlled experiment of an RL-based Dynamic Adaptive Gamification (DAG) system. &
40 university staff members in Malaysia. &
DAG outperformed static gamification in engagement, completion rates (87.6\%), and 7-day retention (46.5\%). &
Direct empirical benchmarking against static gamification using objective telemetry. &
Small sample size; short 2-week intervention window. &
Scaling to Deep RL; testing hybrid systems; broader demographic validation.
\tabularnewline
\hline


Naseer et al. &
Imran &
2025 &
Quasi-experimental mixed-methods testing an adaptive 4-module platform. &
250 undergraduates over 8 weeks. &
Adaptive group showed significantly higher post-test scores, intrinsic motivation, and mastery (82\% vs 64\%). &
Robust sample size; triangulates cognitive and behavioral metrics. &
Single-institution trial; lacks direct physiological emotion sensing. &
Multi-arm decomposition studies; delayed post-testing; sensor-based affective computing.
\tabularnewline
\hline


Nugroho et al. &
Lee &
2025 &
PRISMA systematic literature review. &
20 studies from 7 databases (206 screened). &
Four categories of harm; harm is element-specific; 10.1\% rated points not at all helpful. &
Large, transparent search process covering 2020--2025. &
Qualitative categories only; element combinations not examined. &
No operational harm metric usable as a real-time system constraint.
\tabularnewline
\hline


Edwards &
Lee &
2022 &
Qualitative systematic review. &
40 gamification studies on second language learning. &
Anxiety measured in only 10 studies; competitive elements failed to reduce anxiety in half of the studies using them. &
Direct focus on anxiety, an under-measured outcome. &
Language-learning context; single elements not tested experimentally. &
Controlled tests of which elements raise or reduce anxiety.
\tabularnewline
\hline


Gao &
Lee &
2024 &
Conceptual analysis (position paper). &
No empirical sample; identifies IMI, BPNSFS and PENS instruments. &
Functional significance of an event differs by learner; gradually reduced dose of gamification recommended. &
Grounds harm in established self-determination theory with validated instruments. &
No empirical validation; recommendations not implemented. &
Convert SDT principles into runtime constraints on element selection.
\tabularnewline
\hline


G\"{u}l &
Lee &
2026 &
Qualitative case study. &
113 students in T\"{u}rkiye (30 interviewed). &
Brain rot manifests as attention deficits, procrastination, anxiety and distorted time perception; reward loops identified as a cause. &
Links compulsive engagement to concrete, documentable behaviours. &
Self-reported screen time is biased; strategies not implemented. &
Quantitative prevalence and longitudinal measurement of compulsive use.
\tabularnewline
\hline


Zhang et al. &
Yaw &
2026 &
Quasi-experimental mixed-methods study with pre-test, post-test, questionnaires, and interviews. &
136 valid university students; gamified experimental group vs. traditional control group over 4 weeks. &
Gamified instruction significantly improved learning performance, classroom participation, interest, motivation, and self-efficacy. &
Multidimensional evaluation with control group, pre/post testing, effect-size analysis, and qualitative triangulation. &
Short 4-week intervention; intact-class allocation; possible novelty and social-desirability effects; single institution. &
Longer full-semester evaluations and comparisons of adaptive gamification across broader populations.
\tabularnewline
\hline


Lu et al. &
Yaw &
2024 &
Random-effects meta-analysis with moderator analysis. &
22 empirical studies published from 2010--2023, producing 46 independent effect sizes. &
Overall effect was Hedges' $g=0.740$; knowledge achievement $g=0.841$, motivation $g=0.743$, and thinking skills $g=0.367$. &
Quantitative synthesis of effect sizes across learning outcomes, durations, sample sizes, and game types. &
Focused on AI education and combined educational games, game-based learning, and gamification; high heterogeneity across studies. &
More gamification-specific evidence and long-term studies that evaluate both educational benefits and possible negative effects.
\tabularnewline
\hline


Durmaz et al. &
Yaw &
2022 &
Controlled field experiment comparing redesigned and original gamification elements. &
392 active university students in a one-term course lasting 4 months; 194 experimental and 198 control. &
Redesigned leaderboard and point mechanisms reduced fear of failure and power motives, reduced performance-avoidance goals, and improved theory and final grades. &
Long-duration field study; directly examines psychological effects of leaderboard and point design while other elements were kept unchanged. &
Participants were mainly young Spanish students, limiting generalizability; only selected game elements were redesigned. &
Replication across more diverse populations and further element-level testing of how gamification design affects motivation and harm.
\tabularnewline
\hline


Ojonuba et al. &
Yaw &
2025 &
Quasi-experimental mixed-methods comparison of gamified and non-gamified learning systems. &
60 university engineering students initially enrolled; 53 participants included in final analysis. &
The gamified group achieved better performance, but engagement and motivation did not differ significantly from the non-gamified group. &
Clear gamified vs. non-gamified baseline; evaluates performance, engagement, and motivation using the same course content. &
Short intervention; no random assignment; sample may be underpowered for small effects; multiple game elements were combined simultaneously. &
Longer adaptive studies and factorial or A/B experiments to isolate individual game-element effects and reduce novelty effects.
\tabularnewline
\hline


Ming \& Choo &
Yaw &
2025 &
Cross-sectional study using online questionnaires and learning analytics after gamified flipped-learning activities. &
163 first-year pharmacy students; three escape-room activities conducted during Weeks 9--11. &
Video engagement, lecture preparedness, motivation, enjoyment, and confidence increased after the gamification activities. &
Combines behavioural learning analytics with student-reported motivation and engagement measures. &
Short-term cross-sectional evaluation; no longitudinal assessment; participation before lectures varied. &
Longitudinal evaluation of whether engagement and motivation gains persist over a longer academic period.
\tabularnewline
\hline


L\'opez-Fern\'andez et al. &
Yaw &
2024 &
Cluster-randomized controlled trial with pre-test, post-test, and perception questionnaire. &
217 computer science students; 110 in the traditional-lecture control group and 117 in the LEGO Serious Play experimental group. &
The experimental group achieved greater learning gains and reported higher motivation than the traditional-lecture group. &
Large sample; rigorous randomized comparative design; uses both learning-performance and perception measures with effect sizes. &
Intervention lasted about 1.5 hours and evaluates LEGO Serious Play rather than pure educational gamification. &
Longer-duration comparative studies are needed to determine whether game-related learning effects remain stable over time.
